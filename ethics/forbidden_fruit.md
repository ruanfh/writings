# Genesis forbidden fruit reading

This reading makes no claim to authority.

It is limited strictly to Genesis 2:4–3:24.

## Status: Work in progress

Missing:

- Consequences of eating the fruit

## Symbolism of the senses

| Sense   | Symbolism                      |
| ------- | ------------------------------ |
| Sight   | Self-evident knowledge         |
| Hearing | Socially shared knowledge      |
| Smell   | Intuition about what is hidden |
| Taste   | Destructive analysis           |
| Touch   | Mutual exposure                |

To feel a taste one must chew, destroying the thing being tasted and evaluating its parts.

Note that one can chew to taste then spit, so ingestion is yet another symbol to be examined.

## Symbolism of the serpent

A serpent has the form of a sinuous line.
A line can join two points together while splitting two regions of the plane apart.

What also connects and separates is reason. Reasoning is a way of establishing similarities and differences.

But a sinuous line does so in a way that is not straight.
In other words, a sinuous line uses a path that can forge any separation given two points to interconnect.
Given one similarity, a sinuous line can represent the act of arbitrary selection of facts to deceive.

In fact, the serpent opens dialog with a misdirection that exaggerates the prohibition, inducing resentment:
"Did God really say that you can't eat from **any** fruit of the garden".

## Symbolism of ingestion

To ingest something is to destroy it and use its materials to enhance or degrade oneself.

## Symbolism of the fruit of good and evil

The boundary is: don't eat.
The woman mentions that the fruit shall not even be touched, but the original command was to not eat it.
Permissions that are still granted:
- See
- Talk about
- Smell

So good and evil can be known by:
- Self-evidence, patent knowledge
- Social sharing (although that is the means for the transgression)
- Intuition, latent knowledge

But it shall not be known by:
- Destructive analysis

## Symbolism of clothes

Clothes are associated with protection and social roles.

## Before eating

Before eating, the woman saw that:

- the tree was good for food
- it was pleasant to the eyes
- desirable to make one wise

The tree was good for food means she had the idea of eating the forbidden fruit.
So up to that point they hadn't even considered disobeying God.
It is as if the commandment not to eat from the fruit was taken wholeheartedly.
This idea only appears after the serpent's persuasion.

The channel for corruption is the speech which was the same channel used for the commandment.
Social norms can be both aligned with God or against God. 

The pleasant to the eyes and desirable to make one wise are no longer about contemplative knowledge of good and evil.
The idea of eating is embedded in these statements.

## Symbolism of eating the fruit

They could see and smell the forbidden fruit before eating it.
This means they already knew good from evil long before eating from the forbidden fruit.
Otherwise, how could they know it was wrong to eat from it?

They knew good and evil by evidence, intuition and social sharing.
What they did lack was the possessive knowledge of good and evil that comes from dismantling and re-authoring good and evil.

Note that they could have brought the idea of eating the fruit before God for approval first.
But instead, the woman and the man chose to disobey.
So there is no innocence in their act.

What eating means is first tasting then ingesting.
So first they destroy the whole fruit in order to analyze the parts.
Then they ingest the parts in order to rebuild good and evil from those parts.

That means disassembling good and evil in order to rebuild them according to their own forms.

Once one eats there is no going back.
Once they ate the forbidden fruit it can no longer be extracted out of them.

## Symbolism of the consequences

What happens after they eat the fruit is not that they can arbitrate good and evil voluntarily.

What happens is that their eyes open, meaning they started seeing things as evident that weren't there before.

And what they see is that they are without clothes, which had not been a problem up to that point.
That is telling: they start to feel ashamed of being without clothes.

That can simply mean that they are ashamed from their disobedience.
But there is more to it, because God said that they had become "as one of us".
So there was some truth amidst the serpent's lies.
When it promised that they would become like gods that was actually truth.

But what does "be like gods" mean?
Examining the symbolism of clothes once again may reveal the ability to create arbitrary social norms.
But it is important to note that they didn't voluntarily create that first rule: "one needs clothes in order to be seen".
Instead they enacted it as if it was a necessity.

The power they receive from eating the fruit is actually a curse.
The curse of being bound by arbitrary social norms.
With that curse comes the power to craft and impose one's social norms upon others.
That can be seen as the origin of evil in human actions: self-righteousness.

Another very clear indicator that the new rule is arbitrary is the fact that God does not recognize the rule as valid.
"Who said you were naked?".

Note that the binding is not consciously chosen by them.
Instead they are confused about good and evil.
They can now see (meaning it is evident to them) that they need clothes (social roles), which is not established by God (not natural law).
The new social norms are felt as hallucinations; they can't distinguish them from the truth.

Another point is that this is not the origin of hierarchies; there were already very well established hierarchies before.
God commands man and woman.
Man rule over the animals.
What is new is the inauguration of artificial hierarchies.

## Symbolism of the punishments

Even though the man and the woman now follow arbitrary social norms, they can't escape natural law.
When God punishes them, there is nothing they can do about it but comply.

There are two parts of the punishment:

- The exile and further prohibition of reentering the Garden
- The curses that became the human condition

The first follows naturally from the loss of trust caused by the man and the woman's betrayal of God.

The second shows the weight of their disobedience.
If there were no clothes (no need for protection), no thorns, no pain in childbirth, no death, and so on, then it would be as if there were no sorrow at all.

The curses can be thought of as the origin of pain in a cosmos that was meant to be only pleasant.
And there is a clear causation: God imposes pain upon the man and the woman because they disobeyed.
That causation is the most mysterious part of the whole passage.

One reading is that by challenging God and the structure of good and evil, man and woman brought pain upon themselves by making the cosmos imbalanced.

Note that before they eat the fruit God is always benevolent, and as soon as they eat, God starts to be severe.
But God didn't change; in fact, the man and the woman are the ones who changed by eating the forbidden fruit.

If their sight was now compromised, so was their hearing.
When they hear God cursing them, one might ask: is that what happened, or is it a distortion?
One reading is that their very relationship with God was compromised.

So God was not angry; they were the ones who were scared.
In other words, the consequences are real but not necessarily punishments.
